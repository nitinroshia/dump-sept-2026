# Huge in Japan, Setlog’s Hour-by-Hour Video App Is Coming for the US
Setlog is less than a year old, but roughly 10 million global users send some 60 million day-in-the-life videos on the app every day.

Like pretty much every other Gen Zer, Mon Salipsip starts the day by looking at their phone to see what their close friends have been up to. But the first stop isn’t Instagram or TikTok. It’s Setlog, a social media app that’s made deep inroads with young users in East Asian economies before turning even a year old.

Launched by New York startup New Chat Inc. in December 2025, the app lets groups of friends take two-second clips every waking hour and export them into a collective day-in-the-life video, or vlog. Groups are invite-only, so users control who can view their clips, and videos are taken in-app with no filters or editing. Salipsip, who’s 23 and recently graduated from university in Manila, says the app helps them keep in touch with friends. Some are working their first real jobs, while others pass the time playing video games or doing crafts, but they all make time every hour to document the day on Setlog. “That’s one way to really allow us to connect and be updated on what’s going on, since we’re all in different stages of our lives.”

The app first took off with South Korean users this spring before gaining traction in neighboring countries, especially with the so-called MZ generation (a term combining millennials and Gen Z commonly used in South Korea). Today, roughly 10 million global users send some 60 million videos on the app every day, according to New Chat. While Setlog itself has no algorithmic feed and vlogs are capped at groups of 20 viewers, exported videos have racked up millions of views on other social media platforms.

New Chat founder and Chief Executive Officer Devin Doty, who spent eight years at Snap Inc. as a product designer, says South Korea was the target market for the launch due to a gap in its social media landscape. “People don’t use Snap there,” he says. “They have their legacy messaging apps, and then they have Instagram. So it was interesting to see young people living without a product like Snap, because it’s like, ‘How do you keep up with your close friends when you’re that age?’” The majority of Setlog’s users are now in Japan, with South Korea No. 2 in usage, though close to a third of downloads have been by users in Vietnam, Taiwan, Hong Kong, the Philippines, Thailand, Singapore and Indonesia, according to market researcher AppMagic.

Users say one of the app’s main appeals is privacy. Setlog doesn’t collect data on age or gender. “When people are using an app like this, it is because they’re doing it with their close friends and want that level of privacy,” Doty says. That’s especially appealing to young female users, says Kaito Nishimura, CEO of Nizi to Mangetsu and Inc., a marketing agency in Tokyo that focuses on youth trends. “There’s fear around things like being hit on or having strange photos sent to them,” he says.

Users also like that Setlog shows an unvarnished snapshot of a person’s day. That’s particularly true for fans wanting to glean any intel they can on their favorite stars’ real lives. Doty credits the app’s skyrocketing popularity in Asia to South Korean celebrities using it to document behind-the-scenes looks at their concerts or mundane bits of their days, which are often shared in online fan communities. The chance to get an unfiltered peek into a person’s typical daily life has even turned Setlog into a blind-dating tool for some university students and office workers. Another appeal: It doesn’t require any time-hungry editing skills.

Setlog isn’t the first app to try to show a little more unfiltered reality, rather than the heavily edited videos on infinite scroll elsewhere. Retro, a weekly friends-only photo journal, and even Instagram’s Close Friends feature can give a similar sense of intimacy on the internet, a decidedly unintimate place. The closest analogue is probably BeReal, which exploded in popularity in 2022. BeReal largely faded in the West, but it has continued to see robust growth in Japan. As of June, BeReal had more than 6.5 million active users in the country, up 30% from a year earlier. It has been expanding the number of official accounts by brands and celebrities, with ads now appearing on users’ feeds alongside friends’ posts.

Nishimura says BeReal’s strategic shift to allowing corporate accounts is precisely why users are flocking to Setlog, which is free and has no ads. Nishimura sees Setlog continuing to grow in Japan this year but cautions the app’s future could depend on how it proceeds with monetization. “If they start adding sponsored links and things like that, or the moment ads come in, everyone leaves,” he says.

But Setlog will need to find a way to make money eventually. Doty says he’s considering a subscription-based model over advertising. But before he can worry about profits, New Chat must prove to investors it can sustain Setlog’s rapid growth, he says. It’s still a small operation, with only a handful of employees in New York and Seoul, and Doty, who told a Japanese podcast he was using his personal phone number as the company contact number, says New Chat is working to cut the monthly costs of supporting the app’s operations from around $360,000 to less than half that.

Investors are footing the bills for now: New Chat raised $12.5 million in its seed funding round that closed in September, led by Japan’s SBI Group. Doty says Setlog is aiming to roll out more features this year and is looking at a potential collaboration with a South Korean idol group. The next test is whether Setlog can translate its success in Asia to back home in the US. The app is already available globally, and New Chat has been conducting an experiment with micro-influencer content to gauge appetite in the North American market. “We should be able to see if we can spark the same organic growth flywheel that we have in East Asia, or if we need to spend more to grow,” Doty says.

For Erika Morales, a 30-year-old graphic designer in Seattle who found out about the app from some of her favorite K-pop stars, Setlog offers a low-pressure way to catch up with friends: “It feels more intimate and selective.” She says she wouldn’t mind if Setlog eventually had ads, but she’d stick around and pay a fee only if all her friends did too: “As long as people in my groups use it, I can see myself using it for years.” —With Arielle Busetto and Ririko Tada
