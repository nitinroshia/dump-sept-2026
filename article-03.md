# A £4 Billion Fuel Windfall Offers Hope For Struggling UK Drivers
John Healey is set to reap a £4 billion ($5.3 billion) tax windfall from soaring petrol and diesel prices, putting Britain’s chancellor of the exchequer under growing pressure to announce relief for drivers at his upcoming budget.

Consumers are paying almost £1.75 a liter for petrol, a 31% increase since the start of the Middle East conflict, according to motoring organization RAC. The pain is even worse for users of diesel, with the fuel that powers the transportation industry up 40% to a record £2 a liter.

If these prices stick, the government could collect £3.9 billion more value-added tax in the next 12 months than if prices had remained around their pre-war levels, according to Bloomberg calculations. The estimates assume drivers maintain the quantity of fuel they consume at 2025-2026 levels.

At the Labour Party annual conference this week, Healey and Prime Minister Andy Burnham voiced concern about the pressures on businesses and consumers, who are facing both higher prices at the pump and rocketing gas and electricity bills. In a BBC interview on Wednesday, Burnham said “every possible measure” is being considered but signaled he had little room for giveaways given the state of the public finances.

Lobby groups want the chancellor to extend a freeze on fuel duty into 2027, which would cost over £2 billion, and offer duty rebates for commercial users such as lorries, coaches and vans. Others are urging Healey to go further by cutting VAT on fuel until the energy crisis abates, a potential loss to the exchequer of hundreds of millions a month.

“A reduction in either fuel duties or VAT would cost money but I think this would be at least partially offset by the benefit of reducing the risk of inflation and the risk of a serious reduction in economic activity,” Nick Butler, founding chair of the Policy Institute at King’s College London and a former executive at BP Plc, told Bloomberg.

Motorists are bracing for a fresh shock in January when the government is due to start reversing a 5 pence-per-liter cut to fuel duty that was announced in the aftermath of Russia’s invasion of Ukraine.

Duty is due to rise by 3 pence to 55.95 pence on Jan. 1 and by a further 2 pence on March 1, effectively adding 6 pence to the price of liter of fuel — the equivalent of £3.30 more to fill up a family car.

From April, duty is set to rise in line with the rate of RPI inflation, currently 3.4%. Further pressure could come from US President Donald Trump’s threat to ban diesel exports, with the UK among the biggest buyers.

Britons pay more at the pumps than drivers in most European countries. That’s partly because VAT is charged at 20% on the price including fuel duty. It means that taxes account for 47% of a liter of petrol and 43% for diesel.

The Road Haulage Association said the surge in diesel costs since February means transport firms are now paying over £300 extra a week per vehicle, or £130,000 more every month for those with 100 trucks.

“When the cost of moving goods goes up, prices follow. Where transport firms can’t pass it on, they struggle,” RHA Managing Director Richard Smith said in response to questions from Bloomberg.

The RAC Foundation, a transport policy research group, estimates that the government has collected about £1.27 billion in extra VAT since the start of the war on Feb. 28, although some of that is likely to be claimed back by businesses. It said that total additional VAT paid last Friday alone exceeded £10 million.

The cash boost has provided some leeway for Healey in an otherwise tight budget, with the surge in borrowing costs in recent months estimated to have halved the £23.6 billion buffer against the fiscal rules that existed before the Iran war upended global energy markets.

Earlier this summer, the government said it expected any increase in VAT receipts from higher fuel prices to be offset by lower consumption elsewhere, downplaying the possibility of a windfall.

However, consumers have been more resilient than feared, with households tapping into savings to ride out the shock. Official data show the number of liters consumed since the start of the war is still marginally higher than in the same period of 2025.

In the UK, around one in three cars runs on diesel but trucks are almost entirely reliant on the fuel, as is a significant part of the rail network. The RAC estimates that a third of forecourts are now selling diesel for over £2 a liter.

The government should use its “extremely high” VAT receipts to help motorists, said RAC Head of Policy Simon Williams. Drivers will be watching the October budget “very carefully indeed.”
